== MECH: Regression to the mean, plus choosing to comment at a peak [perception_or_statistical_artifact/strong]
EXPL: Any repeated experience (a meeting, a cafe visit) is part stable signal (how good the friendship or cafe really is) and part noise (mood, sleep, topic, weather, who else was there, a lucky moment). An observation that is extreme because of favourable noise will usually be followed by a less extreme one, even if nothing has changed. That is regression to the mean (Galton 1886). Selection is the key point. People do not say 'this was one of our best meetings' or write a glowing review at random moments. They say it at a peak, so the comment is almost by definition followed by something closer to average. Superlatives make this mathematically sharp. If meeting quality varied at random around a stable level, the chance that the next meeting beats the best of the previous n is only 1/(n+1). After 10 meetings, saying 'best ever' leaves about a 9% chance that the next one tops it. In a warming friendship there is a real upward trend, so the odds are better, but regression still happens around that trend line. Kahneman's flight-instructor case is the textbook example. Instructors saw that praise after an excellent landing was followed by a worse one, and concluded that praise hurt performance. The 'curse' was regression, and the praise caused nothing. The Sports Illustrated cover jinx is the classic popular example: athletes reach the cover after an unusually strong run.
FRIEND: You verbalise closeness ('we've really gotten close', 'one of the best meetings ever') after the meetings that felt exceptionally warm. The next days and meetings are statistically likely to feel less intense: an ordinary coffee, a slower reply, a busier week for the friend. Because the statement and the dip are adjacent in time, the dip looks caused by the statement, just as the instructors' praise looked harmful. The mirror image is a useful test. After a flat or awkward meeting, the next one tends to be better. If you had complained after the flat ones, regression would make complaining look like it 'heals' the friendship.
CAFE: People write a review, especially an enthusiastic one, after a standout visit: perfect coffee, a good table, a friendly barista. Later visits regress toward the cafe's real average, with a different shift, a busy hour or a slightly off batch. The review marks the peak, so the decline 'starts' right after it. This is the Sports Illustrated cover pattern on a small scale.
CAVEAT: The statistics are certain. How much of the user's experience it explains has not been measured and is an inference. The pattern fits the description closely: comments come at peaks, and the 'curse' is a mild 'bir tık' (slight) worsening rather than a collapse. The SI jinx analysis is a magazine analysis, not a peer-reviewed study. Regression does not rule out real effects too. It only means a post-comment dip is expected even without any.
  * [V] Galton, F. (1886). Regression towards mediocrity in hereditary stature. Journal of the Anthropological Institute of Great Britain and Ireland, 15, 246-263. :: Offspring of extreme parents are on average less extreme than their parents. This is the origin of 'regression' as a statistical concept. Galton's causal (hereditary) explanation was later superseded, but the statistical phenomenon is general.
  * [V] Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. Science, 185(4157), 1124-1131. Retold in Kahneman, D. (2011). Thinking, Fast and Slow, ch. 17 'Regression to the Mean'. :: Flight instructors saw that praise after an exceptionally smooth landing was usually followed by a worse landing, and harsh criticism after a rough one by an improvement. They concluded that praise is detrimental. The authors show this is regression toward the mean, so the conclusion is unwarranted. The exact 1974 wording was confirmed only through a secondary quotation.
  * [V] Barnett, A. G., van der Pols, J. C., & Dobson, A. J. (2005). Regression to the mean: what it is and how to deal with it. International Journal of Epidemiology, 34(1), 215-220. (A correction to a formula was published in 2015.) :: A review showing that regression to the mean makes natural variation in repeated measurements look like real change when unusually high or low values are selected. Without a comparison or control it is easily mistaken for a causal effect.
  * [V] Wolff, A. (2002, January 21). That old black magic. Sports Illustrated (magazine analysis, not peer-reviewed). :: Of 2,456 SI covers (1954-2001), 913 (about 37%) were followed by some measurable misfortune for the cover subject. This is widely explained as regression, since athletes make the cover after an unusually good run.

== MECH: Illusory correlation, confirmation bias and selective memory for 'hits' [perception_or_statistical_artifact/moderate]
EXPL: People overestimate the co-occurrence of distinctive events. A meaningful act (saying something emotionally loaded out loud) followed by a meaningful outcome (a cooling) is a vivid, story-like pair that gets encoded and retold. The other cells of the contingency table do not form stories and are forgotten: the times you said it and nothing went wrong, the times things cooled without your saying anything, the times you stayed silent and things stayed good. Judging whether X causes Y requires all four cells, but people overweight the 'X present, Y present' cell. The distinctiveness-based illusory correlation effect is moderate in size and larger when the distinctive outcome is negative. Once the 'jinx' hypothesis exists, confirmation bias steers attention toward evidence that fits it.
FRIEND: 'I told her it was our best meeting, then she was a bit distant for two days' becomes a memorable, explanatory episode. Times you said something similar and the friendship carried on fine, and times a friend got distant with no prior remark, are not filed as jinx evidence. Over months this produces a strong felt rule from what may be zero real contingency.
CAFE: 'I reviewed it, and then the next coffee was bad' is memorable. The many cafes you reviewed that stayed fine, and the cafes that got worse without any review, are not tallied. A cafe you have publicly praised also becomes a distinctive object you pay attention to, so its misses stand out.
CAVEAT: The underlying biases are well established, but no study has tested this exact 'praise then decline' belief in friendships, so applying them here is an extrapolation. The size of the bias in everyday life is uncertain. Fiedler and others offer memory and information-loss accounts of the distinctiveness effect, but all accounts predict the same overestimation here.
  * [V] Chapman, L. J. (1967). Illusory correlation in observational report. Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 6(1), 151-155. :: This paper coined 'illusory correlation'. Observers overestimated how often distinctive or associated word pairs had co-occurred.
  * [V] Hamilton, D. L., & Gifford, R. K. (1976). Illusory correlation in interpersonal perception: A cognitive basis of stereotypic judgments. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 12(4), 392-407. :: When two infrequent things co-occur (a minority group and less frequent negative behaviours), people overestimate their association even though the actual proportions are identical. The search confirmed the authors, year and finding through secondary sources. The journal details are from memory.
  * [V] Mullen, B., & Johnson, C. (1990). Distinctiveness-based illusory correlations and stereotyping: A meta-analytic integration. British Journal of Social Psychology, 29(1), 11-28. :: A meta-analysis finding that the distinctiveness-based illusory correlation is highly significant and moderate in strength, and larger when the distinctive behaviour is negative.
  * [V] Matute, H., Blanco, F., Yarritu, I., Diaz-Lago, M., Vadillo, M. A., & Barberia, I. (2015). Illusions of causality: how they bias our everyday thinking and how they could be reduced. Frontiers in Psychology, 6, 888. :: A review of 20 years of experiments. Anyone can develop illusions of causality under well-known conditions, such as a frequent outcome and attention to coincidences. Explicit scientific, contingency-based thinking reduces them.

== MECH: Tempting-fate intuitions and the feeling that one's own words or thoughts cause outcomes [perception_or_statistical_artifact/moderate]
EXPL: Risen and Gilovich (2008) proposed that actions which 'tempt fate' (presuming a good outcome, boasting) automatically bring the matching negative outcome to mind. That ease of imagining makes the outcome feel more likely. Many people hold this intuition even when they consciously reject it: Risen (2016) calls this 'acquiescence', where people detect that an intuition is irrational but do not correct it. Pronin et al. (2006) showed 'apparent mental causation': when people have thought about an outcome beforehand, they overestimate their causal role in it, even through magical routes such as a 'hex'. Zhang, Risen and Hosey showed that avoidant rituals such as knocking on wood lower people's sense that a jinx will come true. This is the cognitive engine behind 'tahtaya vur' and 'maşallah'.
FRIEND: Saying 'we've gotten so close' or 'best meeting ever' is a textbook tempting-fate act: it presumes the good state will last. It brings 'what if this fades?' to mind, so any later cooling feels expected and therefore caused. Apparent mental causation adds the sense that 'my words did this'. A risk the user should know about is that the anxiety may drive behaviour (over-checking messages, pulling back, overcompensating). That would make it partly a self-fulfilling process, although this step has not been tested directly.
CAFE: Publicly praising a cafe in a review is the consumer version of boasting about good luck. The 'what if it goes downhill now' thought becomes accessible, and the next mediocre coffee confirms it.
CAVEAT: Replication of Risen and Gilovich (2008) is mixed. Many Labs 2 reproduced the basic main effect with a smaller effect size. Many Labs 5 (Mathur et al., 2020), at four sites similar to the original, supported neither the main effect nor the cognitive-load interaction. Swirsky, Fernbach and Sloman (2011) attribute the effect to an illusion of control rather than to availability. Treat it as a plausible, modest intuition, not a strong law. None of these studies tested friendships, so applying them here is an extrapolation. Rituals change felt risk, not real outcomes.
  * [V] Risen, J. L., & Gilovich, T. (2008). Why people are reluctant to tempt fate. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 95(2), 293-307. :: People intuitively judge negative outcomes as more likely after an action that tempts fate. The authors argue that tempting fate makes the negative outcome come to mind more easily, which inflates its perceived likelihood.
  * [V] Mathur, M. B., et al. (2020). Many Labs 5: Registered multisite replication of the tempting-fate effects in Risen and Gilovich (2008). Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 3(3), 394-404. :: A preregistered replication with N = 754 at four similar sites did not support the interaction or the main effect. Six less similar sites gave comparable results. Many Labs 2 had earlier replicated the main effect with a smaller effect size.
  * [V] Zhang, Y., Risen, J. L., & Hosey, C. (2014; online 2013). Reversing one's fortune by pushing away bad luck. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (DOI 10.1037/a0034023). :: After tempting fate, avoidant actions directed away from the self (knocking on wood, throwing a ball) reduced people's expectation that a jinx would come true. Risen cautioned that this changes perceived luck, not actual luck. The volume and issue were not confirmed by search.
  * [V] Pronin, E., Wegner, D. M., McCarthy, K., & Rodriguez, S. (2006). Everyday magical powers: The role of apparent mental causation in the overestimation of personal influence. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 91(2), 218-231. :: People who had thought about an outcome beforehand (evil thoughts toward a 'hexed' person, visualising a peer's basketball success) were more likely to believe they had caused it, including in live field settings and for unwanted outcomes.

== MECH: Contrast effect and a higher reference point after declaring a peak [perception_or_statistical_artifact/moderate]
EXPL: A salient past positive event affects later well-being in two ways. As an 'endowment' it adds to how good things feel. As a 'contrast' it raises the standard against which later experiences are judged, so ordinary events feel worse by comparison (Tversky & Griffin, 1991). Lottery winners in Brickman et al. (1978) took less pleasure in mundane activities. In consumer research, satisfaction depends on performance relative to expectations: the same performance feels disappointing when expectations are higher (Oliver, 1980). Saying the peak out loud, especially as a superlative ('best ever'), fixes it as an explicit benchmark. The next normal meeting or visit is then measured against that benchmark instead of being enjoyed on its own terms.
FRIEND: After 'that was one of the best meetings we've had' or 'we're really close now', the next meeting is judged as 'is it still at that level?' rather than 'was it nice?'. A perfectly good ordinary meeting can then register as a decline. The friendship hasn't changed; the yardstick has. This is a perceptual shift, not a real deterioration, although acting on it could create a real one.
CAFE: Once you have written '5 stars, best flat white in the city', the next visit is graded against the review. On the cafe side, Kovács and Sharkey (2014) found that books which won prestigious awards attracted more readers and then saw their Goodreads ratings fall more steeply than finalists'. They attributed this mainly to a broader, less well-matched audience and to popularity itself, not to the books changing. A single personal review rarely changes a cafe's crowd, so for the user the expectation and contrast part is probably the main factor. Godes and Silva (2012) also found that successive online ratings of the same product tend to decline.
CAVEAT: Tversky and Griffin (1991) is a book chapter with a theoretical model and supporting studies. Brickman et al. (1978) used small samples and its 'adaptation' interpretations have been critiqued. Kovács and Sharkey and Godes and Silva concern crowds of reviewers, not one person's later judgments, so they are analogies here. That labelling a peak raises one's own reference point is a reasonable extrapolation, not a directly tested finding in friendships.
  * [V] Tversky, A., & Griffin, D. (1991). Endowment and contrast in judgments of well-being. In F. Strack, M. Argyle, & N. Schwarz (Eds.), Subjective Well-Being (pp. 101-118). Pergamon. :: A salient past hedonic event affects current well-being both directly (endowment) and through contrast. A past positive event can make the present seem worse by comparison.
  * [V] Brickman, P., Coates, D., & Janoff-Bulman, R. (1978). Lottery winners and accident victims: Is happiness relative? Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 36(8), 917-927. :: Lottery winners were not happier than controls months later and reported less pleasure from ordinary daily activities. This is interpreted as habituation plus contrast with the peak.
  * [V] Oliver, R. L. (1980). A cognitive model of the antecedents and consequences of satisfaction decisions. Journal of Marketing Research, 17(4), 460-469. :: Expectation-disconfirmation model: satisfaction depends on prior expectations and on how far performance falls short of or exceeds them.
  * [V] Kovács, B., & Sharkey, A. J. (2014). The paradox of publicity: How awards can negatively affect the evaluation of quality. Administrative Science Quarterly, 59(1), 1-33. :: Prize-winning books drew more readers after the award, and their Goodreads ratings declined more steeply than those of finalists. The decline was accounted for by a shift in audience tastes and by rising popularity.

== MECH: Negativity bias and heightened vigilance once closeness has been declared [perception_or_statistical_artifact/moderate]
EXPL: Across many domains, including close relationships, bad events, feedback and information carry more psychological weight than equally sized good ones. Negative events also dominate mixed impressions (Baumeister et al., 2001; Rozin & Royzman, 2001). Once something valued has been explicitly claimed ('we're close'), there is something to lose. Attention shifts toward detecting threats to it, and loss-framed vigilance means small negative cues (a short reply, a cancelled plan, a less animated conversation) are noticed more and weighted more heavily than equal positive cues. The same events that would have passed unnoticed before the declaration now register as a 'dip'.
FRIEND: Before saying 'we've gotten close', a friend's two-day silence is just a busy week. Afterwards it is read as possible evidence of cooling, and because bad is weighted more than good, one cool exchange outweighs several warm ones. If the user then reacts (asks 'is everything okay?', withdraws, tries too hard), the perception can turn into a real interpersonal change. That would be a self-fulfilling path, but it is not documented for this exact scenario.
CAFE: After you have praised a cafe publicly, you watch it as its 'endorser': you notice the dirty table, the lukewarm cup, the slow service. One bad visit outweighs several fine ones.
CAVEAT: Negativity bias is well supported in general, but its universality and size are debated, and there are domains with positivity effects. The specific claim that labelling closeness increases monitoring is an extrapolation by analogy. Research on attachment anxiety and relationship vigilance would bear on it more directly but belongs to another cluster. Public commitment can also produce the opposite (consistency, defending one's view), so the direction may vary by person.
  * [V] Baumeister, R. F., Bratslavsky, E., Finkenauer, C., & Vohs, K. D. (2001). Bad is stronger than good. Review of General Psychology, 5(4), 323-370. :: A broad review concluding that bad events have greater impact than good ones across domains, explicitly including close relationship outcomes, information processing and impression formation.
  * [V] Rozin, P., & Royzman, E. B. (2001). Negativity bias, negativity dominance, and contagion. Personality and Social Psychology Review, 5(4), 296-320. :: Proposes a general bias to weight negative entities more heavily (negative potency, negativity dominance), and argues that negative entities are more 'contagious' than positive ones.

== MECH: Fear of happiness and belief that happiness is fragile ('joy is followed by sorrow') [cultural_belief/moderate]
EXPL: 'Fear of happiness' is a measurable belief that great joy or good fortune invites misfortune. Joshanloo validated a short Fear of Happiness Scale across 14 national groups. As recalled from memory, unverified wording, its items include ideas such as preferring not to be too joyful because joy is usually followed by sadness, and disasters following good fortune. A related construct, fragility of happiness (happiness is easily lost), was validated across 15 nations. Joshanloo and Weijers (2014) review cultural sources of 'aversion to happiness', including evil-eye concerns in Iran and neighbouring regions and East Asian norms against displaying happiness. Ji, Nisbett and Su (2001) showed culturally variable lay theories of change: Chinese participants expected more change from an initial state than Americans did. In Turkey, the Fear of Happiness Scale has been adapted (Demirci et al., 2016), with good reliability and negative correlations with life satisfaction. The belief is present and measurable in Turkish samples, though Turkey was not one of the 14 original nations.
FRIEND: The user's frame ('verbalising the goodness curses or poisons it') is close to the content of these scales. Someone higher in fear of happiness or fragility beliefs will expect a downturn after a high and will read ordinary fluctuation as confirmation. The belief does not cause the dip, but it supplies the explanation ('I jinxed it') and may increase anxious monitoring.
CAFE: The same lay theory ('when something is too good, it turns') applies to a cafe that has just been praised: the expectation of reversal is in place before the next visit.
CAVEAT: The scales are well validated, but the studies are mostly cross-sectional surveys of students. There is no evidence that fear of happiness predicts actual friendship decline after positive statements, so that link is speculative. Country-level differences are averages with large individual variation. Ji et al. studied Chinese and American participants, and mapping their results to Turkish culture is an extrapolation.
  * [V] Joshanloo, M., Lepshokova, Z. K., Panyusheva, T., et al. (2014; online 2013). Cross-cultural validation of Fear of Happiness Scale across 14 national groups. Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology, 45(2), 246-264. :: The Fear of Happiness Scale showed measurement invariance and good properties across 14 nations (including Iran, Japan, India, Pakistan, Russia, Brazil, Kenya, Kuwait and New Zealand, but not Turkey). Fear of happiness was present everywhere, higher in some non-Western samples, and associated with lower life satisfaction. The co-author list beyond Joshanloo was not confirmed by search.
  * [V] Joshanloo, M., & Weijers, D. (2014). Aversion to happiness across cultures: A review of where and why people are averse to happiness. Journal of Happiness Studies, 15(3), 717-735. :: A review documenting beliefs in many cultures that happiness invites misfortune, envy (including the evil eye) or moral costs. It notes that some people do not treat happiness as a supreme value.
  * [V] Joshanloo, M., Weijers, D., Jiang, D.-Y., Han, G., Bae, J., Pang, J. S., et al. (2015). Fragility of happiness beliefs across 15 national groups. Journal of Happiness Studies, 16(5), 1185-1210. :: The Fragility of Happiness Scale (happiness is fleeting and easily lost) showed good cross-cultural measurement properties and related to psychosocial variables in the expected directions. It is linked to fear of happiness.
  * [V] Demirci, I., Ekşi, H., Kardaş, F., & Dinçer, D. (2016). Mutluluk Korkusu Ölçeği'nin Türkçe formunun geçerlik ve güvenirliği [Validity and reliability of the Turkish form of the Fear of Happiness Scale]. Kastamonu Education Journal. :: In 283 Turkish university students, the five-item scale was unidimensional (loadings .71-.92) with α = .90. Fear of happiness correlated negatively with life satisfaction and subjective happiness.

== MECH: Evil-eye (nazar) beliefs as a cultural heuristic for managing envy [cultural_belief/weak]
EXPL: The evil-eye belief is widespread around the Mediterranean, the Middle East and Turkey. One cross-cultural analysis (Gershman, 2015) argues it arose as a heuristic for avoiding others' envy in unequal societies with vulnerable wealth. Displaying or announcing good fortune draws envy, so norms of concealment and protective phrases ('maşallah', 'nazar değmesin', the nazar boncuğu) developed. Experimental work shows that people who expect to be maliciously envied act to appease potential envier(s) (van de Ven et al., 2010). In Turkey a 2025 survey (N = 601) found that 58% endorsed belief in the evil eye, and that benign and malicious envy were among the strongest predictors. So for a Turkish speaker, 'praising something good out loud without a protective phrase puts it at risk' is a culturally familiar schema that is available whenever a dip follows praise.
FRIEND: Within the cultural frame, saying 'we've become so close' without a 'maşallah' feels like exposing the friendship to nazar. That schema is ready to explain any later cooling. There is also a possible real channel, separate from magic: closeness announced in front of others (a shared friend group, social media) can trigger third-party envy or a sense of exclusion. This is speculative and does not apply to remarks made privately to the friend.
CAFE: Praising a business publicly is the classic 'nazar' situation in Turkish folk thinking: a shop that is praised is thought to lose its luck. The schema provides a ready explanation for a later off day.
CAVEAT: Gershman's analysis is cross-cultural and correlational, and summaries disagree on its sample size (76 vs. 186 societies). Van de Ven et al. tested fear of envy in Dutch participants, not nazar beliefs as such. The Turkish survey is correlational, so it shows prevalence and correlates, not causation. Applying any of this to friendship dips is speculative. Its main value is to explain why the 'curse' interpretation feels so natural to the user, not to show that a curse occurs.
  * [V] Gershman, B. (2015). The economic origins of the evil eye belief. Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, 110, 119-144. :: The evil-eye belief is more common in societies with greater wealth inequality and vulnerable material wealth (for example, agro-pastoral societies). This is consistent with the belief serving as a heuristic to avoid destructive envy.
  * [V] van de Ven, N., Zeelenberg, M., & Pieters, R. (2010). Warding off the evil eye: When the fear of being envied increases prosocial behavior. Psychological Science, 21(11), 1671-1677. :: Participants who received an undeserved advantage, and so expected malicious envy, were more likely to help the potentially envious person. They did not become more helpful in general. The authors interpret this as an appeasement strategy.
  * [V] Türkarslan, K. K., & Kozak, E. D. (2025). Exploring the evil eye beliefs: A quantitative study. Spiritual Psychology and Counseling, 10(2), 313-328. :: In 601 participants in Türkiye, 58.24% endorsed belief in the evil eye, and women scored higher. Benign and malicious envy, non-religious/non-spiritual orientation and non-religious paranormal beliefs explained about 60.5% of the variance.

== MECH: Counter-evidence: no evidence that words or praise causally jinx outcomes, and superstition affects feelings rather than results [counter_evidence/moderate]
EXPL: Within this cluster, the studies that come closest to testing magical causation point the same way. Jinx rituals change perceived risk, not outcomes (Zhang et al.). The felt power of one's thoughts over outcomes is an illusion of apparent mental causation (Pronin et al.). The tempting-fate intuition is itself fragile in large preregistered replications (Many Labs 5). Even the reverse claim, that good-luck superstitions improve performance (Damisch et al., 2010), failed in high-powered replications (Calin-Jageman & Caldwell, 2014). In Tversky and Kahneman's flight-instructor case, the apparent harm of praise vanished once regression was taken into account. None of this proves that explicit closeness talk can never change a real interaction, which the other clusters examine. It does mean the 'curse' pattern is fully expected from statistics and perception even if speech has zero causal effect.
FRIEND: There is no evidence that saying 'this was a great meeting' sends bad luck to the friendship. The dip after a peak would happen anyway, and expressing warmth is not the cause. The user should not stop expressing appreciation because of this pattern. The other clusters cover evidence that expressing positive feelings usually helps relationships.
CAFE: A review cannot change the coffee. The later decline is regression plus raised expectations, with real changes such as staff turnover or crowding that would have happened anyway.
CAVEAT: 'Absence of evidence' arguments are only as strong as the tests. No study has directly tested whether verbalising closeness predicts later friendship decline beyond regression. Real interpersonal effects of superlative or premature closeness talk (felt pressure, mismatched intimacy pacing) are plausible and belong to the 'real interpersonal dynamics' cluster.
  * [V] Calin-Jageman, R. J., & Caldwell, T. L. (2014). Replication of the superstition and performance study by Damisch, Stoberock, and Mussweiler (2010). Social Psychology, 45(3), 239-245. :: Two high-powered replications found no performance benefit from activating a good-luck superstition, contrary to the original 'Keep your fingers crossed!' findings. The volume and pages are from memory. The replication outcome was confirmed via the FORRT Replication Atlas.
  * [V] Risen, J. L. (2016). Believing what we do not believe: Acquiescence to superstitious beliefs and other powerful intuitions. Psychological Review, 123(2), 182-207. :: People can recognise that a superstitious intuition is irrational and still act on it ('acquiescence'), especially when the case feels special and ignoring reason costs little. This explains why the jinx feeling persists despite knowing better.
  * [V] Mathur, M. B., et al. (2020). Many Labs 5: Registered multisite replication of the tempting-fate effects in Risen and Gilovich (2008). Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 3(3), 394-404. :: The tempting-fate effect on judgments was not supported at the replication sites. Even the intuition that tempting fate raises negative outcomes is not robust, let alone any real effect on outcomes.

== MECH: Timing confound: closeness-talk gets said at peaks and turning points, so a dip follows whether or not you speak [perception_or_statistical_artifact/weak]
EXPL: Everyday talk between friends is mostly gossip, making plans, joking, catching up, small talk and recapping the day. Explicit talk about the relationship is the exception. It tends to come out at turning points: Baxter & Bullis (1986) found that about half of the turning points couples reported involved explicit relationship talk. So people say 'this was one of our best meetings' or 'we've gotten really close' right after an unusually good meeting or when the relationship is shifting. An unusually high point is usually followed by more ordinary ones (regression to the mean), and turning points are unsettled times anyway. Because the statement is rare, vivid and emotionally loaded, memory uses it as the marker for whatever comes next. The result looks like 'I said it, then it got worse', even though the statement only marks the peak and did not cause the decline.
FRIEND: You say the superlative after an exceptional meeting, which is the top of the curve. The next few days are average, a bit flat, or busy for the friend. Next to the peak that feels like a fall, and the statement is the easiest thing to blame. The same flatter days after a great meeting where you said nothing are less likely to be noticed or remembered as a 'curse'.
CAFE: People write reviews, especially glowing ones, after their best visit. Later visits are closer to the cafe's real average. Some decline is also real, from staff turnover or more crowding. The review stands out in memory and gets blamed for both.
CAVEAT: Known: relationship talk clusters at turning points (romantic sample, retrospective interviews), and everyday talk is mostly small talk. Extrapolated: that this produces an illusory 'jinx' in friendships. No study tests the jinx perception directly. Regression to the mean and illusory correlation are probably covered in more depth by the perception/statistics cluster.
  * [V] Baxter, L. A., & Bullis, C. (1986). Turning points in developing romantic relationships. Human Communication Research, 12(4), 469-493. :: 80 partners from 40 romantic relationships described 759 turning points, sorted into 26 types. About half involved explicit metacommunication (relationship talk), and how likely relationship talk was depended on the type of turning point. Relationship talk therefore clusters at transitions.
  * [V] Goldsmith, D. J., & Baxter, L. A. (1996). Constituting relationships in talk: A taxonomy of speech events in social and personal relationships. 'Involving' talk, such as relationship talk, was less typical. This is an inference from the taxonomy: the sources do not report how often relationship talk occurs.

== MECH: Relationship-state talk is a 'taboo', high-stakes kind of speech, especially in developing and ambiguous relationships [real_interpersonal_dynamic/moderate]
EXPL: Baxter & Wilmot (1985) interviewed 90 people about topics that were off limits in their opposite-sex relationships. The state of the relationship was the most widespread taboo of the six categories they found. Informants held a negative view of relationship talk as destructive, inefficient, futile and risky. Later work extended topic avoidance to same-sex and cross-sex friendships, with 'relationship issues' as one avoided domain and 'relationship protection' as one motive (Afifi & Guerrero, 1998). Cross-sex friendships are thought to carry higher uncertainty and more topic avoidance (Afifi & Burgoon, 1998). The point is that saying 'we've gotten close' is not just a compliment. It is metacommunication: a move that proposes a new definition of the relationship. That changes the conversation from 'enjoying each other' to 'stating what we are'. In friendships this matters more because there is no formal ceremony such as 'going out' or engagement to mark closeness. An explicit statement is one of the few markers available, so it carries more weight than it seems to. A proposed definition calls for a response. If the other person is less sure, or simply doesn't talk this way, they may answer awkwardly or vaguely, and the speaker can read that as cooling. Notably, informants' lay view that 'talking about the relationship is risky' looks like the folk belief behind 'nazar değmesin' or knocking on wood. The jinx belief may partly be a cultural form of a real communicative norm.
FRIEND: In a friendship that is warming but not yet defined, 'this was one of our best meetings' or 'we've really gotten close' asks for confirmation. A friend who is reserved, has a different pace, or is less sure where things stand may give a lukewarm reply or a slightly more careful next interaction. That is a small, real ripple, not a curse.
CAFE: Only a loose analogy. A public review changes your role from anonymous regular to someone who has publicly 'defined' the relationship (fan, critic, known reviewer). Staff may treat you differently, and your later visits are judged against what you wrote.
CAVEAT: Known: avoidance of relationship talk is well documented in interview and self-report studies, mostly of US college students, many opposite-sex or romantic relationships, and mostly from the 1980s and 1990s. The 1985 'taboo' list was updated in a 2016 thesis that found partly different categories. Not known: whether POSITIVE relationship talk, as opposed to 'where is this going?' talk, causes harm. The taboo concerns relationship-state talk in general, often uncertain or problem-focused. Acitelli's spouse studies point the other way: relationship talk was rated favorably. I could not read the full text of Afifi & Burgoon (1998), so its specific findings are unverified.

== MECH: Relational turbulence: the middle zone of growing intimacy is the most reactive phase, with or without the statement [real_interpersonal_dynamic/weak]
EXPL: Relational turbulence theory (Knobloch & Solomon) says that transitions toward intimacy raise relational uncertainty ('do they feel the same? what are we?') and interference: as lives start to intertwine, the other person begins to disrupt routines and plans. Both make people more emotionally and cognitively reactive, so small irritations feel bigger and ambiguous signals are read as threats. In dating couples, Solomon & Knobloch (2004) found a small curvilinear link between intimacy and negative appraisals of irritations, which fits the theory's prediction of more reactivity at moderate intimacy. Uncertainty and interference did NOT statistically explain that pattern, and a later longitudinal test gave the feedback loop only partial support. Relational uncertainty is also linked to more topic avoidance and more indirect communication. The user describes exactly this zone: 'we know each other, but have started meeting a lot.' That is the transition phase in which the friendship is most sensitive to small signals.
FRIEND: The weeks when closeness is rising quickly are when a late reply, a cancelled plan, or a friend's busy week feels most loaded. This predicts that saying 'I love our friendship' to a long-standing close friend would not produce the same 'curse'.

== MECH: Dialectical pendulum: after peak connection and openness, a swing toward autonomy and privacy is normal and gets misread as decline [real_interpersonal_dynamic/weak]
EXPL: Relational dialectics theory (Baxter, 1988, 1990; Baxter & Montgomery, 1996) holds that close relationships are driven by ongoing tensions between opposing needs: connection vs. autonomy, openness vs. closedness, predictability vs. novelty. These are managed over time rather than resolved, and one common pattern is alternating between the poles. Altman, Vinsel & Brown (1981) reframed social penetration theory in the same way: openness is regulated alongside withdrawal and privacy, not as a one-way march toward intimacy. Rawlins (1992) identified friendship-specific dialectics, including the freedom to be independent vs. the freedom to be dependent, and expressiveness vs. protectiveness. Friendships are voluntary and loosely structured, so these tensions are managed implicitly. An intense, very open meeting followed by a statement of closeness sits at the 'connection/openness' pole. A few days with less contact, lighter talk, or the friend attending to the rest of their life is the expected counter-swing. It is not evidence of damage.
FRIEND: A 'best ever' meeting is a peak of connection and disclosure, and the statement marks it. The friend, and you, may then need some autonomy or 'protective' distance to digest it. That shows up as a quieter week, which feels like a curse. It may also be speculatively possible that naming the peak makes the connection pole more noticeable and so prompts the counter-swing a little sooner. No study has tested this.
CAFE: Weak analogy. Your own novelty vs. predictability tension: after the 'honeymoon' with a place, the same experience feels less special (habituation), regardless of the review.
CAVEAT: Dialectical theory is influential but mainly interpretive. Empirical support comes from retrospective interviews, mostly with romantic partners. Baxter (1990) studied 106 romantic partners: contradictions appeared in about three-quarters of reported stages, openness-closedness was reported more early on, and satisfaction depended on how contradictions were managed rather than whether they were present. Rawlins (1992) is a qualitative and theoretical book. Searches did not confirm the specific terms 'cyclic alternation' and the named praxis patterns; these come from my knowledge of Baxter's work. The idea that verbalizing a peak speeds up the swing is pure extrapolation.

== MECH: Expectancy violation and face threat: direct, unequivocal affection between platonic friends flatters but also imposes, and can prompt a small compensatory step back [real_interpersonal_dynamic/moderate]
EXPL: Expectancy violations theory (Burgoon) holds that unexpected behavior draws attention and gets evaluated. Whether the result is positive depends on how the behavior is interpreted and how rewarding the communicator is. In an experiment with 40 pairs of adult platonic friends, Floyd & Voloudakis (1999) found that unexpected changes in affectionate involvement shifted recipients' cognitive assessments and behavior, and that reactions differed by whether affection went up or down. Erbert & Floyd (2004), applying politeness theory to affectionate messages between platonic friends, found that the most direct, unequivocal affectionate messages gave the most support to the receiver's positive face (feeling liked) but also posed the most threat to their negative face (autonomy, freedom from imposition). They also found a curvilinear relation between directness and receivers' assessments. A superlative ('one of the best meetings ever') or a relationship claim ('we've gotten so close') is a direct, unequivocal affectionate message. It implicitly asks the other to reciprocate, match the level, and keep it up. A friend who feels that pressure may compensate by pulling back slightly. Andersen et al. (1998) found a mix of reciprocal and compensatory responses to higher immediacy in opposite-sex friend pairs. The size of this effect is easy to overstate: Burgoon & Hale (1988) found that increased immediacy between friends did not raise ratings but did not markedly lower them either. Decreased immediacy reliably hurt.
FRIEND: Your warm superlative is received as flattering but also as a small imposition: 'now I'm expected to feel and say the same, and to keep this level up.' Depending on the friend's style, they may answer with a light joke, a deflection, or slightly more reserve over the next days. That is compensation for autonomy, not dislike. It is more likely when the statement is about 'us' than about your own enjoyment, and more likely with reserved or newer friends.
CAFE: Weak analogy. Staff who read a glowing review about themselves may feel watched or obliged to live up to it, which can make the service self-conscious. This is speculative.
CAVEAT: Known: the experiments and scenario studies are about nonverbal or affectionate involvement and judged messages, mostly with US students. Extrapolated: applying them to one verbal statement about the relationship and a multi-day 'decline'. The effects are probably small, and the main finding is that more warmth is mostly NOT punished. I could not read the full texts, which are scanned PDFs, so the direction-specific results of Floyd & Voloudakis come from the abstract only.

Violations were judged differently depending on whether affection increased or decreased. The authors read this as support for expectancy violations theory.

== MECH: Pacing mismatch: verbal labels of closeness can run ahead of the friend's own sense of the friendship [real_interpersonal_dynamic/weak]
EXPL: Social penetration theory (Altman & Taylor, 1973) describes relationships deepening gradually in breadth and depth of disclosure, with 'depenetration' (backing off) when costs outweigh rewards. Classic disclosure studies suggest that timing and fit matter. Cozby (1972) argued that liking rises with disclosure intimacy only up to a point. Wortman and colleagues (1976) found that an intimate disclosure made early in a 10-minute conversation could lead to less liking than the same disclosure made late, when negative traits were attributed to the early discloser. Friendship formation also takes a lot of time together: Hall's survey estimated roughly 40-60 hours for casual friendship and more than 200 hours for close friendship. 'We've gotten really close' is a disclosure about your view of the relationship. If the other person's internal timeline is slower, the label can feel premature: not unwelcome, but out of step. They may then implicitly slow down to restore the pace that feels right to them.
FRIEND: Frequent meetings can compress the calendar, but each person's sense of closeness may grow at different rates. If you call it 'one of our best' or 'really close' before the friend has reached that point, they may tap the brakes: fewer messages, lighter topics. This is about matching pace and will mostly be invisible in established friendships.
CAFE: Not applicable beyond a loose sense of over-committing publicly ('best cafe in the city') before you have enough visits to know its real average.
CAVEAT: The 'too-early or too-intimate' findings are old, conditional, and from short lab conversations with strangers. Cozby's curvilinear finding was contradicted by at least one later study. The Collins & Miller (1994) meta-analysis found that, overall, people who make intimate disclosures are liked MORE, with some negative findings under specific conditions. Wortman et al.'s effect depended on what observers attributed to the discloser. The search confirmed the article and abstract, but not the author list. Hall's hour estimates come from self-reports and the figures vary across summaries. Applying any of this to a verbal relationship label is extrapolation.

== MECH: Speaker's own post-statement vigilance and 'secret testing' (self-fulfilling withdrawal) [self_fulfilling_process/speculative_extrapolation]
EXPL: Saying 'we've gotten close' is a bid that leaves the speaker exposed. The speaker's own relational uncertainty rises ('did they feel the same? was that too much?'). People under relational uncertainty tend to gather information indirectly rather than ask. Baxter & Wilmot (1984) documented 'secret tests' such as endurance, separation, triangle, third-party and indirect-suggestion tests, and a later study found more testing in early courtship. A common form is to stop initiating and see whether the other person reaches out, which is a separation or endurance test. Uncertainty is also linked to more topic avoidance and more indirect messages. The behavior that reliably harms interactions in expectancy-violation research is reduced immediacy: detachment, less involvement (Burgoon & Hale, 1988). So the speaker's own anxiety after the statement (holding back, waiting for proof, reading neutral signals as rejection) can produce the cooling that then 'confirms' the jinx. If the speaker also believes in nazar or jinxing, the belief feeds the vigilance.
FRIEND: After saying it, you may feel you've 'used up' something or exposed yourself. You then watch the friend's replies closely, wait for them to text first, and act a little more guarded. The friend experiences you as slightly less warm or less available and adjusts. Two people each waiting for the other produces the dip. A quick check: count who initiated contact in the days after such statements, compared with normal weeks.
CAFE: After reviewing, you visit as an evaluator ('is it still as good as I said?'), looking for flaws you would not have noticed before. A shift in attention rather than a real change in the cafe.
CAVEAT: Secret tests are documented in interview studies of romantic and opposite-sex relationships. That positive relationship talk specifically triggers testing or withdrawal by the SPEAKER in friendships has not been studied. It is a plausible chain built from separate findings. Self-fulfilling-prophecy work, such as rejection sensitivity, is probably covered more directly by another cluster.
  * [V] Baxter, L. A., & Wilmot, W. W. (1984). 'Secret tests': Social strategies for acquiring information about the state of the relationship. Human Communication Research, 11(2), 171-201. :: People often learn where a relationship stands through indirect strategies (endurance, separation, triangle, third-party and indirect-suggestion tests) rather than by asking directly. A later study found more testing in early courtship and a positive association with jealousy. Details come from secondary sources.
  * [V] Burgoon, J. K., & Hale, J. L. (1988). Nonverbal expectancy violations: Model elaboration and application to immediacy behaviors. Communication Monographs, 55(1), 58-79. :: Decreased immediacy (detachment) violations produced negative evaluations with both friends and strangers, while increased immediacy did not. The relevance here is that pulling back is more damaging than over-warmth.

== MECH: Counter-evidence: expressing positivity, openness and assurances generally strengthens relationships, including friendships [counter_evidence/strong]
EXPL: Taken as a whole, the communication literature strongly contradicts a general 'saying it jinxes it' rule. Collins & Miller (1994) meta-analyzed three disclosure-liking effects and found all three significant: people who make intimate disclosures are liked more, people disclose more to those they like, and people come to like those they have disclosed to. They described these as a dynamic, mutually reinforcing system. In friendships, positivity, supportiveness, openness and interaction are key maintenance behaviors that predict satisfaction and commitment at the pair level (Oswald, Clark & Kelly, 2004). A meta-analysis of relationship maintenance, mostly romantic, found positivity and assurances (explicitly signalling commitment and the relationship's future) to be among the strongest correlates of satisfaction and commitment (Ogolsky & Bowers, 2013). Escalating, reciprocal, increasingly personal disclosure produces closeness even between strangers (Aron et al., 1997). So the base rate is that warm statements help. Any 'curse' is more likely the timing artifact, normal oscillation, or a fit and pacing issue than the content of the praise.
FRIEND: Over months, friends who regularly hear that they are valued tend to feel more satisfied and committed. A single dip after a statement is noise against that trend. The useful question is not whether you said it, but how and when, and whether you stayed consistently warm and available afterward.
CAFE: Positive reviews generally help a business and do not make the product worse. Any decline after a review is better explained by regression to the mean, changes in staff or crowds (popularity effects), or your raised expectations.
CAVEAT: The meta-analyses are mostly correlational and cross-sectional: satisfied people may express more positivity, rather than positivity causing satisfaction. The maintenance meta-analysis is mostly romantic. Aron et al. produced short-term closeness between strangers in a lab, not lasting friendships. No study directly tests explicit superlative praise of a meeting in new friendships against subsequent contact. I did not confirm the exact effect sizes.
  * [V] Collins, N. L., & Miller, L. C. (1994). Self-disclosure and liking: A meta-analytic review. Psychological Bulletin, 116(3), 457-475. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.116.3.457 :: Significant effects for all three: intimate disclosers are liked more, people disclose more to those they like, and people like those they have disclosed to. Effects were moderated by study paradigm, type of disclosure, and the discloser's gender. Together they form a dynamic interpersonal system.
  * [V] Oswald, D. L., Clark, E. M., & Kelly, C. M. (2004). Friendship maintenance: An analysis of individual and dyad behaviors. Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, 23, 413-441. :: Identified four friendship maintenance behaviors: positivity, supportiveness, openness and interaction. Best friends performed more of them. Within pairs, each person's self-reported maintenance predicted the pair's satisfaction and commitment.
  * [V] Ogolsky, B. G., & Bowers, J. R. (2013). A meta-analytic review of relationship maintenance and its correlates. Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, 30, 3-29. doi:10.1177/0265407512463338 :: 35 studies with 12,273 reports. Mostly romantic samples. The page range is from memory.

== MECH: Intimacy equilibrium and compensation after an intimacy spike [real_interpersonal_dynamic/moderate]
EXPL: Equilibrium theory (Argyle & Dean 1965) proposes that each pair settles at a comfortable level of intimacy, where the pull toward affiliation balances avoidance forces such as fear of intimacy. When one person sharply raises intimacy, the other may compensate by lowering it to restore that balance. Later models refined this. In Patterson's (1976) arousal-labeling model, a change in intimacy produces arousal: labeled positively (liking, warmth), it leads to reciprocation; labeled negatively (pressure, embarrassment, anxiety), it leads to compensation, meaning pulling back. Cappella & Greene's (1982) discrepancy-arousal model says that a moderate gap from what a person expects produces positive arousal and reciprocation, while a large gap produces negative arousal and compensation. So a big, sudden jump ('this was one of the best meetings ever', 'we've become really close') is the case most likely to trigger compensation. The data are mixed: increases in warmth are often reciprocated, especially in established relationships. Compensation is a possible response, not the default one.
FRIEND: A friendship that is warming up is still settling on its intimacy level. An intense meeting followed by an explicit verbal step-up is a large jump above what the friend expected. If the friend reads their own reaction as warmth, they reciprocate and nothing bad happens. If they read it as pressure or 'too much, too fast', they may quietly re-balance over the next few days: slower replies, fewer invitations, a more casual tone. That re-balancing could look like a 'curse'. The model predicts the dip is small and temporary, a return to the friend's comfort level rather than a rejection.
CAFE: Weak analogy only. A cafe does not regulate intimacy in response to a review. The parallel is limited to a regular customer who becomes 'too visible' to staff after praising them publicly, which some staff may find awkward.
CAVEAT: The evidence base is mostly laboratory studies of nonverbal immediacy (gaze, distance, touch, vocal warmth), often among strangers or romantic couples, from the 1960s to the 1990s. Andersen et al. (1998) found a mix of reciprocity and compensation when friends raised their immediacy. Guerrero & Burgoon (1996) found that partners mostly reciprocated increases in involvement. Applying these models to verbal closeness declarations in new friendships is an extrapolation. Equilibrium theory has also been criticized for assuming that intimacy levels stay static.

Confirmed via secondary sources only; I did not read the original.
  * [V] Patterson, M. L. (1976). An arousal model of interpersonal intimacy. == MECH: Attachment avoidance: defensive deactivation when closeness becomes explicit [real_interpersonal_dynamic/moderate]
EXPL: In Mikulincer & Shaver's model, people high in attachment avoidance manage their attachment needs with 'deactivating' strategies: they keep distance, play down their need for others, and stay self-reliant. Rising intimacy and dependence can trigger these strategies. Experiments show that avoidant people steer away from situations that would reveal how others feel about them (Beck & Clark 2009). They also judge others' kindnesses as less freely given, which works as a defense against growing intimacy; a friend condition showed the same pattern (Beck & Clark 2010). An explicit statement like 'we've gotten really close' is exactly the kind of closeness and dependence cue that could set off these defenses. Importantly, avoidant people are not harmed by positive experiences as such. Stanton et al. (2017) found that they benefit from them, with less negative affect and higher relationship quality. The trigger is therefore more likely the felt pressure and dependence implied by labeling the closeness than the positivity itself.
FRIEND: If the new friend is fairly independent or private (a common trait), hearing 'we've become really close' or 'that was one of the best meetings ever' makes the closeness salient and implies a dependence they did not choose. They may respond by quietly creating distance for a few days. The more avoidant the friend, the more likely this is, and the less it says about whether they actually like the user. The same friend may enjoy the meetings themselves and still become uneasy when the closeness is put into words.
CAFE: Not applicable. A business does not have attachment defenses toward a reviewer.
CAVEAT: The evidence that avoidant people defend against intimacy cues is fairly consistent, but most studies involve romantic couples or primed avoidance in student samples. No study I found directly tests the effect of verbally declaring closeness in a new friendship, so that link is an extrapolation. I could not verify a study I expected to cite on avoidance and relationship-focused disclosure ('Tan, Overall & Taylor 2012'), so I left it out. Stanton et al. (2017) is a counterweight: positive, intimacy-promoting experiences help avoidant people over time.

== MECH: Declaring closeness exposes a gap in perceived closeness ('state of the relationship' talk) [real_interpersonal_dynamic/moderate]
EXPL: Two people's sense of how close they are often differs. In Almaatouq et al.'s (2016) survey data, only about half of self-reported friendships were reciprocated, and people were poor at telling which of their friendships were mutual. Talk about the state of a relationship is also one of the most avoided topics. In Baxter & Wilmot's (1985) interviews it was the most common taboo topic, which participants saw as risky and potentially destructive, and in cross-sex friendships it was the topic most often avoided (Afifi & Burgoon 1998). Saying 'we've gotten really close' or 'this was one of the best' turns a comfortably vague state into an explicit claim the other person has to match, qualify or dodge. If their felt closeness is lower, or they simply haven't put a label on it, the moment can feel awkward, and they may respond with a hedge or a lighter tone. The speaker may read that as cooling. One complication: the 'liking gap' (Boothby et al. 2018) shows that people usually underestimate how much their conversation partners liked them, and this held among dorm mates over a year. So the gap is not necessarily that the friend likes the user less than the user thinks; it may only be that the friend is less ready to name it.
FRIEND: In a new friendship each person is still privately estimating where things stand. A superlative or closeness statement invites the friend to say where they stand. If they respond more mildly than the user did ('haha yes it was fun'), the user may experience a letdown and start reading the following days as cooler. If the friend's own closeness was lower, the explicit claim can make them feel they owe a level of closeness they don't yet feel, and they adjust their behavior to match their real level. In both cases the statement didn't create the gap; it revealed a gap that was already there, or forced someone to name it.
CAFE: Partial analogy. A glowing review is a public 'state of the relationship' statement. It changes the user's expectations of the cafe, so the gap between expectation and experience grows, even though the cafe has no way to 'reciprocate'.
CAVEAT: There is good evidence that friendship asymmetry exists and that relationship talk is avoided. Almaatouq et al. used a small class sample (84 students) plus other self-report datasets, and their reciprocity rate depends on where the friendship threshold is set. No study I found shows that declaring closeness causes the other person to pull back, so that causal link is an extrapolation. The taboo-topic studies are older, interview-based and mostly about opposite-sex relationships. The liking gap is robust across five studies and has extensions to groups, and it cuts against the assumption that the friend secretly likes the user less.

== MECH: Risk regulation: naming closeness raises the stakes, and low-self-esteem partners may discount superlatives [real_interpersonal_dynamic/moderate]
EXPL: Murray, Holmes & Collins's (2006) risk-regulation model holds that people constantly balance two goals: seeking closeness and protecting themselves from rejection. The more dependent someone becomes, the more rejection would cost, so people who doubt they are valued tend to pull back to protect themselves at exactly the moments when dependence rises. Superlative praise also reads differently depending on self-esteem. Marigold et al. (2007) found that people with low self-esteem get less security from a partner's compliments unless they are led to think about what the compliment means at a more abstract level. Wood et al. (2005) found that low-self-esteem people respond to success with more anxiety and negative thoughts. A peak experience can feel like a standard they now have to keep meeting.
FRIEND: There are two pathways. (1) The friend: if they are unsure of their own worth, 'one of the best meetings ever' can feel like too much to live up to, or simply implausible, so it doesn't land as reassurance and may even make them anxious or a bit self-protective. (2) The user: saying out loud that the friendship has become important increases their own felt dependence, so any small dip afterwards feels more threatening, and they may protect themselves (become less open) or seek reassurance more. Either path can produce a slightly cooler few days after the declaration.
CAFE: Weak analogy. After a public rave review the user is more invested in the cafe staying good, so small declines feel more salient. This is a stakes effect on perception, not a change in the cafe.
CAVEAT: Risk regulation is well supported in romantic relationships and largely in North American samples. Its application to verbal closeness statements in new friendships is an extrapolation. Marigold et al. studied compliments between romantic partners, and in their data compliments were not harmful; low-self-esteem people just benefited less unless the compliment was reframed. Wood et al. is about personal success, not relationship praise.
  * [V] Murray, S. L., Holmes, J. G., & Collins, N. L. (2006). Optimizing assurance: The risk regulation system in relationships. Psychological Bulletin, 132(5), 641-666. :: People balance seeking closeness against minimizing the likelihood and pain of rejection, through cognitive, affective and behavioral 'if-then' rules. Those who doubt a partner's regard protect themselves when dependence or risk becomes salient.
  * [V] Marigold, D. C., Holmes, J. G., & Ross, M. (2007). More than words: Reframing compliments from romantic partners fosters security in low self-esteem individuals. == MECH: Rejection sensitivity and attachment anxiety: hyper-monitoring after a high point, leading to self-fulfilling withdrawal or neediness [self_fulfilling_process/moderate]
EXPL: Rejection-sensitive people anxiously expect, readily perceive and overreact to rejection (Downey & Feldman 1996). They read intentional rejection into ambiguous or mildly insensitive behavior. In Downey et al.'s (1998) longitudinal diary study, relationships of highly rejection-sensitive people were more likely to break up, even controlling for partners' rejection sensitivity, satisfaction and commitment. The paper frames this as a self-fulfilling prophecy in which expecting rejection elicits behavior that brings it about. Attachment anxiety works similarly: daily perceived conflicts weigh more heavily on anxious people's satisfaction and closeness (Campbell et al. 2005). Lemay & Clark (2008) showed a further loop: people who believe they have revealed their insecurities come to doubt the authenticity of their partner's positive regard, which keeps the insecurity going, and partners do in fact 'walk on eggshells' around them.
FRIEND: After saying 'we've become really close', the user has more to lose. They may then watch the friend more closely: reply speed, tone, whether the next invitation comes. Ordinary fluctuations (a busy day, a short message) get read as signs of the 'curse'. Acting on that reading, by sending extra messages, asking 'is everything ok?', joking defensively, or pulling back first to save face, can create the distance it was meant to prevent. The friend may also start to feel they must be careful around the user, which makes interactions feel slightly less natural.
CAFE: After a glowing review the user watches for flaws (a slower coffee, a less friendly barista) and notices things that were always there. Here only the perception part applies; there is no behavioral loop with a cafe.
CAVEAT: The perceptual effect (rejection-sensitive people perceiving ambiguous behavior as rejection) is well replicated across studies. The full self-fulfilling chain comes mostly from romantic couples and US college samples. Applying it to the user specifically depends on how rejection-sensitive or anxiously attached they are, which we do not know. My knowledge is that the 1998 lab study linked high-rejection-sensitivity women's negative behavior during conflict to their partners' anger, but I did not re-verify that detail in this search. The breakup percentages reported in popular summaries were also not checked against the original paper.

== MECH: Expectancy confirmation: jinx-fear changes the speaker's warmth, and the friend responds in kind (acceptance prophecy in reverse) [self_fulfilling_process/moderate]
EXPL: Expectations shape behavior, and behavior shapes how others respond. In Snyder, Tanke & Berscheid's (1977) classic study, men who believed they were talking to an attractive woman behaved in ways that led her to come across as warmer and more sociable to independent judges (behavioral confirmation). Stinson et al. (2009) showed that people who expect acceptance behave more warmly, and that warmth leads others to accept them; warmth explained the effect better than four alternatives. The reverse follows: expecting rejection or decline leads to cooler, more guarded behavior, which invites a cooler response. Stinson et al. (2011) found that a brief self-affirmation made insecure people more secure and more relaxed and warm in social behavior up to two months later, which shows the loop can be interrupted. Risen & Gilovich (2008) explain why a 'jinx' belief makes decline feel likely: an action that 'tempts fate' calls the bad outcome to mind, which makes it seem more probable. So a person who believes 'saying it ruins it' will, right after saying it, expect decline and may act in ways that help bring it about.
FRIEND: The user already holds the belief that saying the friendship is going well jinxes it. After saying it, they likely feel 'now it will go wrong'. That expectation can make them a little more guarded or tentative, less spontaneous, slower to suggest the next meeting, or overly careful. Any of these lowers the warmth the friend receives, and the friend matches it. The jinx then 'comes true' through the user's own behavior, not through the words. Each confirmation strengthens the belief, so the cycle reinforces itself. Cultural rituals ('maşallah', knocking on wood, 'nazar değmesin') may partly work by reducing this anxiety so the person can keep acting normally.
CAFE: After the review the user expects decline, notices and remembers the misses, and may visit less often or with a more critical eye. This is mainly expectation-driven perception plus selective memory, with no feedback loop to the cafe.
CAVEAT: The acceptance-prophecy mechanism (warmth mediating the effect) is well documented in lab settings. Expectancy effects in general tend to be real but modest. Some reviewers, notably Lee Jussim, argue that self-fulfilling prophecies in everyday life are usually small; I did not search-verify that critique here. The 1977 Snyder study is a classic with limited exact replications; a web replication exists but I did not check its results. Self-affirmation interventions have had mixed replication in other areas. Risen & Gilovich's accessibility account has been challenged by Swirsky et al. (2011), who argue for an illusion-of-control explanation. Applying this chain to the user is an extrapolation.
  * [V] Snyder, M., Tanke, E. D., & Berscheid, E. (1977). Social perception and interpersonal behavior: On the self-fulfilling nature of social stereotypes. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 35(9), 656-666. :: Men who believed a phone partner was attractive elicited warmer, more sociable behavior from her, as rated by independent judges, showing that a perceiver's expectation can confirm itself through the interaction.
  * [V] Stinson, D. A., Cameron, J. J., Wood, J. V., Gaucher, D., & Holmes, J. G. (2009).   * [V] Stinson, D. A., Logel, C., Shepherd, S., & Zanna, M. P. (2011). == MECH: Reactance to implied obligations: a closeness label can feel like new communal expectations [real_interpersonal_dynamic/speculative_extrapolation]
EXPL: Psychological reactance (Brehm 1966) is the motivation to restore a freedom that feels threatened; a meta-analysis models it as a mix of anger and counter-arguing (Rains 2013). Close friendships run on communal norms, in which partners are expected to respond to each other's needs rather than keep score (Clark & Mills 1979). Declaring 'we're really close now' can be heard as an implicit renegotiation: more availability and responsiveness are now expected. For someone who values autonomy, or isn't sure they want that level yet, the label can feel like a constraint on their freedom to set the pace. A mild, often unconscious push back to reassert freedom (a later reply, declining the next plan) is the predicted reaction.
FRIEND: The friend enjoyed the meeting but hears in 'we've become so close' an expectation to meet often, reply quickly, and take on best-friend obligations. Pausing for a few days lets them feel they are choosing the friendship rather than being drafted into it.
CAFE: Not applicable in any meaningful way. A review does not impose obligations on a cafe in the reactance sense.
CAVEAT: Reactance research is mostly about persuasive and health messages, not friendship talk, and communal-norm studies use manipulated relationship expectations in the lab. I found no study testing reactance to closeness labels in friendships. Treat this as a plausible hypothesis that fits the pattern, not an established finding.
  * [V] Brehm, J. W. (1966). A theory of psychological reactance. New York: Academic Press. And Rains, S. A. (2013). The nature of psychological reactance revisited: A meta-analytic review. Human Communication Research, 39(1), 47-73. :: Reactance is the motivation to restore a threatened freedom. Rains's meta-analysis of 20 studies supported an 'intertwined' model in which reactance combines anger and negative cognitions.
  * [V] Clark, M. S., & Mills, J. (1979). Interpersonal attraction in exchange and communal relationships. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 37(1), 12-24. :: Distinguished communal relationships, where benefits respond to the other's needs, from exchange relationships, where benefits are repaid. The same behaviors (prompt repayment, requests) affected attraction differently depending on which kind of relationship was desired.

== MECH: Counter-evidence: expressing appreciation and closeness usually helps, and people underestimate how much [counter_evidence/strong]
EXPL: The broader evidence points the other way: telling people you value them, or that you enjoyed time with them, generally lands better than the speaker expects. In Kumar & Epley (2018), people writing gratitude letters underestimated how happy and surprised recipients would feel and overestimated how awkward recipients would feel; expected awkwardness held people back from expressing gratitude. Zhao & Epley (2021), across nine experiments, found that people underestimate the positive impact of genuine compliments, and this underestimation is a barrier to giving them. Closeness also tends to grow through gradual, reciprocal escalation of self-disclosure (Aron et al. 1997, the '36 questions' procedure). Even avoidant people benefit from positive relationship experiences (Stanton et al. 2017), and partners in established relationships mostly reciprocated increases in warmth (Guerrero & Burgoon 1996). The liking gap (Boothby et al. 2018) means the friend probably likes the user more than the user thinks. So a dip after positive talk is not the expected average effect. When it happens, the more likely causes are the form of the statement (superlative, label, pressure to match), the friend's individual style, the user's own post-statement behavior, or plain regression after a peak.
FRIEND: The user should not conclude that expressing positivity harms friendships. Specific, sincere appreciation ('I really enjoyed today, I love talking with you') is probably more welcome than the user fears. What may occasionally backfire is a relationship-status claim or superlative that asks the friend to calibrate. And the jinx belief itself, by making the user guarded afterwards, may cost more than the statement does.
CAFE: For a cafe, a positive review usually helps the business; any perceived decline afterwards is more plausibly regression to the mean or heightened expectations, which another cluster covers.
CAVEAT: Kumar & Epley and Zhao & Epley measured recipients' immediate feelings, not friendship trajectories over the following days. Aron et al. (1997) produced temporary closeness among strangers in the lab. I did not verify the results of Sprecher et al. (2013) on disclosure roles, so I did not cite them. Still, the direction of the evidence is consistent: expressing appreciation is undervalued, not dangerous.

  * [V] Zhao, X., & Epley, N. (2021). Insufficiently complimentary: Underestimating the positive impact of compliments creates a barrier to expressing them.   * [V] Aron, A., Melinat, E., Aron, E. N., Vallone, R. D., & Bator, R. J. (1997). The experimental generation of interpersonal closeness: A procedure and some preliminary findings. == MECH: Reference-point / expectation-contrast effect after a superlative ('best meeting ever' becomes the new yardstick) [perception_or_statistical_artifact/moderate]
EXPL: How good an experience feels depends on how it compares with a reference point, not only on how good it is in absolute terms. Expectation-disconfirmation theory (Oliver 1980) treats satisfaction as a function of expectation plus how far performance confirms or falls short of it. Calling a meeting 'one of the best ever' turns an unusually high peak into the expectation. Retrospective judgments of an episode also lean heavily on its most intense moment and its ending, with duration counting for little (the peak-end rule, Kahneman et al. 1993). So a vivid peak stays in memory as the comparison point. The hedonic adaptation literature (e.g., Sheldon & Lyubomirsky 2012, the Hedonic Adaptation Prevention model) adds that the boost from a positive change erodes along two paths: positive experiences become less frequent or less novel, and aspirations or expectations rise. Together these predict that the next ordinary, good meeting will feel like a step down even if nothing in the relationship has changed. The superlative itself does not damage anything. It raises the bar the next meeting is judged against, and putting it into words probably makes that bar more salient and easier to remember.
FRIEND: After you say 'this was one of our best meetings', the next coffee, which is just pleasant, gets silently compared to that peak and reads as 'something is off'. The friend may be exactly as warm as usual. Your comparison standard is what moved. The 'curse' is the gap between a peak-based expectation and a normal-range experience.
CAFE: This is almost exactly Oliver's original consumer-satisfaction domain. You write the 5-star review at or near the peak visit. That visit becomes the expectation, and later normal visits produce negative disconfirmation ('it's not as good as it was').
CAVEAT: The general principles (reference dependence, expectation-disconfirmation, peak-end, hedonic adaptation) are well established, mostly in consumer, hedonic and pain research. No study we know of has tested whether saying a superlative about a friendship meeting lowers how the next meeting is rated, so the application to friendships is an extrapolation. This mechanism overlaps with regression to the mean (people tend to make superlative statements after unusually good meetings, which are statistically likely to be followed by more average ones). That statistical artifact is covered in another cluster.

This is the foundation of expectation-confirmation theory.
  * [V] Kahneman, D., Fredrickson, B. L., Schreiber, C. A., & Redelmeier, D. A. (1993). When more pain is preferred to less: Adding a better end. Psychological Science, 4(6), 401-405. :: Most participants chose to repeat a longer cold-water trial that ended less painfully over a shorter one. Retrospective evaluations were driven by peak and end moments, with duration having little effect (peak-end rule).
  * [V] Sheldon, K. M., & Lyubomirsky, S. (2012). The challenge of staying happier: Testing the Hedonic Adaptation Prevention model. == MECH: Analyzing or explaining a positive experience dampens its glow (the 'pleasure paradox') [perception_or_statistical_artifact/weak]
EXPL: Work by Wilson, Gilbert and colleagues suggests that the sense-making we apply to a positive event, explaining it and making it understandable, tends to make it feel more ordinary and shortens the good feeling. In Wilson et al. (2005), positive moods lasted longer when people stayed uncertain about a positive event than when it was explained, and participants wrongly predicted the opposite. Lyubomirsky, Sousa & Dickerhoof (2006) found an asymmetry: writing or talking about negative experiences helped, but for the happiest life experiences, privately thinking or replaying them was linked to higher well-being than writing or talking about them. Systematically analyzing them was the most costly. Turning a magical evening into an explicit, verbal verdict ('this was the best, we've really gotten close') may therefore 'explain away' some of its emotional charge for the speaker.
FRIEND: Saying 'we've become so close' or 'that was the best meeting' turns a felt, somewhat mysterious warmth into a labeled, explained fact. In the following days your own sense of the friendship can feel flatter, and you may attribute that flatness to the friendship or the friend. The change may be inside your own processing, not in the relationship.
CAFE: Writing a review forces you to analyze why the cafe was great (the coffee, the light, the staff). The cafe loses some of its unexplained charm, and the next visit feels more ordinary.
CAVEAT: These are lab and short-term studies with modest samples. To our knowledge they have not been widely replicated. In Lyubomirsky et al. (2006), 'talking' meant talking into a recorder, not sharing with the person involved. That is very different from telling a friend, and capitalization research (see the counter-evidence mechanism) shows that sharing good news with a responsive listener increases positive affect. So the dampening effect probably applies to analytic, explanatory talk, not to warm sharing or savoring. Applying it to friendships is a speculative extrapolation. This is an intrapersonal effect: it would change the speaker's feelings, not the relationship.
  * [V] Lyubomirsky, S., Sousa, L., & Dickerhoof, R. (2006). The costs and benefits of writing, talking, and thinking about life's triumphs and defeats. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 90(4), 692-708. :: For negative experiences, writing or talking beat merely thinking. For the happiest experiences, private thinking or replaying was associated with higher life satisfaction than writing or talking, and analyzing happy moments was associated with lower well-being than replaying them.
  * [V] Wilson, T. D., Centerbar, D. B., Kermer, D. A., & Gilbert, D. T. (2005). The pleasures of uncertainty: Prolonging positive moods in ways people do not anticipate. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 88(1), 5-21. :: Across three experiments, positive moods lasted longer when people stayed uncertain about a positive event than when it was explained. People predicted that certainty would feel better. The authors argue that sense-making processes reduce the pleasure we get from positive events.

== MECH: Natural friendship trajectory: closeness talk tends to happen at the steepest part of a curve that must flatten, and contact naturally fluctuates [perception_or_statistical_artifact/moderate]
EXPL: Early friendship is a phase of rapid change: lots of new self-disclosure, novelty and 'self-expansion' (learning a new perspective through the other person). Self-expansion theory (Aron, Paris & Aron 1995, studied in romantic love) shows that the early phase of closeness brings rapid, energizing change in the self-concept. That rate of change cannot stay high, because each new meeting adds less novelty than the first ones did. Hall's (2019) study of adults who had recently moved and of first-year students suggests that closeness accumulates slowly over shared, mostly leisure, time (press summaries give roughly 40-60 hours for a casual friend, 80-100 for a friend, and over 200 for a good friend). So there are long stretches where closeness grows only slowly. Friendships also need ongoing, costly maintenance through contact, and they are more sensitive than kin ties to drops in contact (Roberts & Dunbar 2011, 2015). Ordinary life constraints (work, exams, travel, other friends) make contact rise and fall. The moment you feel 'we've gotten really close' and say so is, almost by definition, the end of a burst of fast growth. A slower patch is the expected next step whether or not anything is said, and that timing makes the statement look like the cause.
FRIEND: You speak up at the high point of the honeymoon phase ('we're meeting so often, we've gotten so close'). The following week the friend is busy or the meeting is merely nice. That is the normal leveling of a new friendship plus ordinary scheduling noise, but because it comes right after the statement it feels like a jinx. Without the statement the same plateau would probably have happened, just less noticeably.
CAFE: A newly discovered cafe has novelty value that adapts away with repeated visits. Cafes also change naturally over time (staff turnover, menu changes, crowds after becoming popular). The review just falls at the end of the novelty peak.
CAVEAT: Hall (2019) studied relationships that persisted during the observation window. Its hour figures differ across press summaries and should be quoted as approximate. We did not access the full text, and the volume and pages are from memory. Aron et al. (1995) studied falling in love, not friendship, so applying its 'rapid early self-expansion' to friendships is an extrapolation, although self-expansion theory has been applied to friendships. Roberts & Dunbar's samples are small (e.g., about 25 students over 18 months in the 2011 study). The claim that closeness statements cluster at growth peaks is our inference and has not been tested.
  * [V] Hall, J. A. (2019). How many hours does it take to make a friend? Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, 36(4), 1278-1296. (Published online 2018; volume and pages from memory.) doi:10.1177/0265407518761225 :: Two studies (355 adults who had recently moved and 112 first-year university students tracked over time) linked hours spent together, especially leisure time such as hanging out and joking rather than working together, to the move from acquaintance to casual friend, friend and close friend. Press summaries give roughly 40-60, 80-100 and over 200 hours respectively. Closeness builds gradually, not in a straight line from one great meeting.

(The kin vs. friend maintenance-cost conclusion was confirmed by search. The detail that closeness fell with reduced contact is from memory.)

== MECH: Jinx-fear leading to guarded behavior and behavioral confirmation (plus the 'liking gap') [self_fulfilling_process/moderate]
EXPL: Expectations about how a relationship will go can produce that outcome through one's own behavior. In the classic behavioral-confirmation experiment by Curtis & Miller (1986), participants led to believe an interaction partner liked them behaved more warmly (more self-disclosure, a more positive tone), and the partner then actually liked them more. Those led to believe they were disliked behaved more coldly and drew less liking. If someone believes that naming a friendship's goodness invites misfortune, saying it can trigger anticipatory anxiety: watching for signs of cooling, holding back on initiating contact, or over-reading a short reply. That guardedness can in turn make the friend a little less warm. The 'liking gap' feeds this: after conversations, people systematically underestimate how much their partner liked them and enjoyed the conversation. In first-year dorm-mates the gap persisted for months (Boothby et al. 2018). Someone already primed to expect a curse will read ambiguous signals in the most pessimistic way.
FRIEND: After saying 'we've gotten really close', part of you waits for the curse. You notice the friend took 3 hours to reply, you initiate less so as not to 'jinx' it further, or you are a bit more reserved at the next meeting. The friend responds to your reserve with some reserve, and the expectation confirms itself. Meanwhile, according to the liking-gap research, the friend probably likes you more than you think.
CAFE: After posting a glowing review you may go in expecting the experience to live up to it, or to be recognized. You become a more critical observer of small flaws, which lowers your experience even if the cafe has not changed.
CAVEAT: Behavioral confirmation is a well-documented phenomenon in general, but the specific chain 'superlative statement, then jinx anxiety, then guardedness, then actual cooling' has not been tested. It is an extrapolation and depends on the person actually holding the superstitious expectation. We could not confirm Curtis & Miller's journal volume and pages by search (believed to be JPSP, 51(2), 284-290), only its design and finding through a secondary source. The liking gap is well replicated in early-acquaintance settings and narrows as relationships mature.

(Volume and pages from memory, not confirmed by search.) :: Participants randomly led to believe they were liked by an interaction partner behaved more warmly (e.g., more self-disclosure, a more positive manner) and were in turn liked more. Believing one is disliked produced the reverse, a self-fulfilling prophecy in liking. (Design and finding confirmed via a secondary description.)
  * [V] Boothby, E. J., Cooney, G., Sandstrom, G. M., & Clark, M. S. (2018). The liking gap in conversations: Do people like us more than we think? Psychological Science, 29(11), 1742-1756. :: People systematically underestimated how much their conversation partners liked them and enjoyed their company. This held for lab strangers, for workshop attendees, and for first-year dorm-mates over several months as they formed new relationships. Preoccupation with one's own perceived missteps contributes to the gap.

== MECH: COUNTER-EVIDENCE: Expressing appreciation, gratitude and liking helps relationships, and people systematically underestimate how much [counter_evidence/strong]
EXPL: A robust research program shows that people hold back positive expressions because they underestimate how much recipients value them and overestimate how awkward recipients will feel. Kumar & Epley (2018) found this for gratitude letters, Zhao & Epley (2021) for compliments across nine experiments, and Boothby & Bohns (2020) for brief compliments to strangers. In brand-new relationships, a simple thank-you made recipients see the expresser as warmer and made them more willing to continue the acquaintance by leaving contact details (Williams & Bartlett 2015). Expressing liking also tends to be reciprocated, because it signals benevolent intentions toward the other person (Montoya & Insko 2008). The overall pattern is that telling a new friend you enjoy them is, on average, one of the things that builds the friendship, not a threat to it.
FRIEND: The data predict that when you tell a friend 'that was a great meeting' or 'I really feel we've gotten close', the friend usually feels more positive and warmer toward you than you expect, not burdened. If a curse really followed every such statement, it would run against a large body of evidence. That makes it much more likely that the dips you notice come from the other mechanisms (contrast, the natural trajectory, regression, your own vigilance) than from the statement itself.
CAFE: Staff and owners tend to value a compliment or good review more than the reviewer expects. Nothing in the evidence suggests that praise causes a business to decline.
CAVEAT: Most studies use one-off expressions such as letters, notes and compliments, often to strangers or acquaintances, with short-term outcomes like the recipient's mood and willingness to affiliate. Long-term friendship outcomes are less directly tested. Zhao & Epley and Kumar & Epley come from one research program, though Boothby & Bohns independently found a similar underestimation. Montoya & Insko's Study 1 had N = 52. Reciprocity of liking has a long research history, but expressed liking works best when it feels genuine and specific to the person (see the form mechanism).

Psychological Science, 29(9), 1423-1435. doi:10.1177/0956797618772506 (volume and pages from memory) :: People who wrote gratitude letters underestimated how surprised and positive recipients would feel and overestimated how awkward recipients would feel. Expected awkwardness predicted lower willingness to express gratitude.
  * [V] Zhao, X., & Epley, N. (2021). Insufficiently complimentary?: Underestimating the positive impact of compliments creates a barrier to expressing them. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 121(2), 239-256. :: Across nine experiments, compliment givers underestimated how positive recipients would feel and overestimated their awkwardness, partly by underestimating how competent and warm the compliment would be perceived to be.   * [V] Williams, L. A., & Bartlett, M. Y. (2015). Warm thanks: Gratitude expression facilitates social affiliation in new relationships via perceived warmth. The effect was mediated by perceiving the thanker as warm. This is direct evidence that expressing positivity helps new relationships start.
  * [V] Montoya, R. M., & Insko, C. A. (2008). Toward a more complete understanding of the reciprocity of liking effect. European Journal of Social Psychology, 38(3), 477-498. :: An admirer's expressed attraction signals benevolent intentions toward the target, and those perceived intentions mediate reciprocated affective and behavioral attraction (Study 1, N = 52). The authors interpret this through interdependence theory.

== MECH: COUNTER-EVIDENCE: Gratitude 'finds, reminds and binds', and positivity is itself a core friendship maintenance behavior [counter_evidence/moderate]
EXPL: Algoe's find-remind-bind theory holds that experiencing and expressing gratitude helps people find good relationship partners, reminds them of the value of existing ones, and binds them closer through mutually responsive behavior. In a peer, friendship-like setting, gratitude felt during a week of benefits predicted later relationship outcomes (Algoe, Haidt & Gable 2008). Expressing gratitude to a relationship partner also raised the expresser's own sense of the relationship's communal strength over time (Lambert et al. 2010). In friendship maintenance research, 'positivity' (being cheerful and pleasant, and expressing enjoyment of the friendship) is one of four core maintenance behaviors, alongside supportiveness, openness and interaction, and maintenance behaviors predict friendship satisfaction and commitment (Oswald, Clark & Kelly 2004). Positive expression is part of how friendships are kept alive, not a hazard to them.
FRIEND: Telling your friend you value the time together is exactly the 'positivity' that maintenance research links to more satisfying, committed friendships. Gratitude research suggests it also strengthens your own sense of the bond. Stopping such expressions out of fear of jinxing would more likely weaken the friendship over time.
CAFE: Less directly applicable, since a cafe is not a communal relationship. The analog is that regular, appreciative customers tend to form warmer ties with staff over time.

== MECH: COUNTER-EVIDENCE with a real-dynamic twist: capitalization (sharing good things), where the listener's response determines the effect [counter_evidence/strong]
EXPL: Telling others about positive events ('capitalization') raises positive affect and well-being beyond the event itself, and perceived active-constructive (enthusiastic, engaged) responses predict greater intimacy and relationship satisfaction (Gable et al. 2004). Responses to good news predicted relationship well-being and breakup two months later more strongly than responses to bad news did (Gable, Gonzaga & Strachman 2006). Experiments with strangers or confederates show that enthusiastic responses to shared good news build trust and prosocial feelings toward the responder (Reis et al. 2010). The twist is that the benefit depends on the response. If the listener responds passively ('yeah, it was nice') or awkwardly, the sharer can feel deflated. Saying 'that was one of the best meetings ever' is in effect a capitalization attempt about the relationship itself. If the friend answers with less enthusiasm, for example because they are more reserved, the superlative seems too strong to match, or they feel put on the spot, the mismatch can sting. It may then color the following days, even though both people still like each other.
FRIEND: In general, sharing 'I loved today' should strengthen the friendship, especially if the friend responds warmly. What may feel like a curse is sometimes a mismatch at that moment: your enthusiasm met a milder response. You read the gap as cooling, and that can start a small mutual pulling back. The useful thing to watch is the friend's response style, not whether you spoke.
CAFE: Weak analog: a public review is not a conversation and gets no immediate response, so this mechanism mostly does not apply to the cafe.
CAVEAT: The main capitalization findings are well replicated, but mostly in romantic couples and college students. Reis et al. (2010) extends them to new acquaintances. Studies concern sharing personal good news (a promotion, a good grade), not statements about the relationship itself. That meta-level statements carry extra risk when met with a mismatched response is our extrapolation. For Gable et al. (2004), search confirmed the citation, and the findings are summarized from knowledge and from the authors' later work.

Perceiving partners' responses as active-constructive was associated with greater intimacy and relationship satisfaction.

== MECH: Form matters: specific, other-praising, present-focused appreciation vs. global relationship labels and superlatives [real_interpersonal_dynamic/weak]
EXPL: The evidence supports expressing positivity, but some forms are better supported than others. (1) Other-praising beats a global verdict: in gratitude, praising the other person's actions and qualities ('you were so thoughtful to...') was the active ingredient linked to the recipient feeling understood, loved and positive. Describing one's own benefit was not (Algoe, Kurtz & Hilaire 2016). (2) Explicit 'state of the relationship' talk is the most commonly avoided topic in opposite-sex relationships, including friendships, and is seen as risky, futile or destructive (Baxter & Wilmot 1985). Friendships run largely on implicit rather than explicit relational definitions, so a label like 'we've become really close' can raise unwanted questions ('what does this obligate me to?', 'is this becoming romantic?'). (3) Selectivity matters: liking aimed specifically at one person is reciprocated, while indiscriminate liking is not and even predicts being liked less (Eastwick et al. 2007). Specific appreciation signals that the liking is about this person. (4) The gain-loss effect (Aronson & Linder 1965): evaluations that rise from neutral to positive produced more liking than uniformly high ones, and a drop from positive was most damaging. Going to the top of the scale early ('best ever') leaves no room for 'gain' signals, so anything later that is merely 'nice' can read as a 'loss'. Relatedly, with children, inflated praise ('incredibly beautiful') backfired for those low in self-esteem, plausibly because it implies a very high standard to keep meeting (Brummelman et al. 2014). Superlatives and premature labels can create a sense of obligation and comparison. Specific, present-focused appreciation ('I really enjoyed today, especially our talk about X') gets the benefits without those costs.
FRIEND: 'This was one of the best meetings ever' and 'we've gotten really close' are global verdicts and relationship labels. They can put mild pressure on the friend to live up to the bar, to define the relationship, or to match your intensity. That is a plausible real, small interpersonal cost, especially early in a friendship or in a cross-sex friendship. 'I really enjoyed this, you're fun to talk to about X' keeps the warmth and drops the ranking and the definitional question.
CAFE: A superlative review ('best cafe in the city') sets a public bar that later visits are judged against, and for the reviewer it fixes the reference point at the top. A specific review ('loved their filter coffee and quiet corner') is more informative and creates less contrast.
CAVEAT: None of these studies directly compared 'superlative/global' and 'specific' positive statements between adult friends, so this mechanism is an extrapolation from adjacent findings. Algoe et al. (2016) used romantic couples and compared other-praising with self-benefit expressions, not superlatives with specifics. Baxter & Wilmot (1985) is qualitative (90 interviews) and limited to opposite-sex relationships; a later thesis update included friendships and found partly overlapping topics. Eastwick et al. (2007) is romantic speed-dating. The gain-loss effect was studied mainly in the 1960s-70s, with relatively few studies and more support for the gain effect than the loss effect. Brummelman et al. (2014) concerns children and is a far extrapolation, offered only as an illustration.

Social Psychological and Personality Science, 7(7), 658-666. :: In video-recorded expressions of gratitude between romantic partners, other-praising (acknowledging and validating the giver's actions and qualities) was linked to the benefactor's perceived responsiveness, positive emotion and feelings of love.   M stretch_but_reasonable: Analyzing or explaining a positive experience dampens its glow (the 'pleasure paradox') || This is the weakest mechanism in the cluster and borders on overreach for the core scenario. The manipulations were explaining WHY a positive event happened (Wilson et al.) and solitary, repeated analytic writing or talking into a recorder (Lyubomirsky et al.). Telling a friend 'that was the best meeting' is an evaluation shared with a responsive person. That is capitalization or savoring-by-sharing, which the evidence says AMPLIFIES positive affect. It applies only if the user goes on to ruminate analytically ('why was it so good? what does it mean?'). The category also needs a fix: this is a real change in the speaker's own feelings, closer to (c) than to a statistical artifact. Better to borrow from Bar-Anan et al. (2009): uncertainty after the statement can intensify NEGATIVE feelings too, such as anxiety about how it landed.
  M stretch_but_reasonable: Natural friendship trajectory: closeness talk tends to happen at the steepest part of a curve that must flatten, and contact naturally fluctuates || The core argument is a sound timing and selection argument: statements are made at peaks, and plateaus or dips follow regardless. It is probably one of the best explanations. The cited evidence is indirect, though. Aron et al. is romantic. Hall (2019) mostly shows closeness accumulating with hours, not a burst followed by a plateau. Roberts & Dunbar track months, not days. More direct friendship evidence exists: Hays (1985, JPSP 48(4), 909-924), tracking new college friendships over a term, reported (from memory, worth checking) that friendships which became close were differentiated within the first weeks, and that the breadth of interaction later leveled off or fell while intimacy kept rising. Relational-dialectics work (connection versus autonomy) gives a real interpersonal reason for a pull-back after intense closeness. The final answer should say the day-level fluctuation claim is common sense, not a finding from these studies.
  M stretch_but_reasonable: Jinx-fear leading to guarded behavior and behavioral confirmation (plus the 'liking gap') || Behavioral confirmation and the liking gap are well established, and the chain is plausible for a person who actually holds the jinx expectation. The user's own report ('a feeling that it curses it') suggests they do. The full chain is untested. Better-matched evidence than Curtis & Miller alone: Stinson et al. (2009), where anticipated acceptance led to warmth and then to acceptance; Downey et al. (1998), the rejection-sensitivity self-fulfilling prophecy; and Murray's risk-regulation model, in which felt vulnerability leads to self-protective distancing. Risen & Gilovich (2008) on 'tempting fate' explains why the jinx intuition grips people: after tempting fate, the bad outcome comes to mind more easily and feels more likely. The liking gap mostly applies to early acquaintance, which fits a warming-up friendship.
  M sound: COUNTER-EVIDENCE: Expressing appreciation, gratitude and liking helps relationships, and people systematically underestimate how much || Sound as a base-rate argument: a large body of evidence says expressing positivity is welcomed more than expressers predict, which makes a systematic curse unlikely. The caveat should be explicit, though. Nearly all of these studies involve one-off thanks for a specific kindness or compliments about a person's traits, mostly to strangers or acquaintances. None tests meta-statements about the relationship itself ('we've gotten really close'), which communication research treats as a distinct and riskier kind of talk (Baxter & Wilmot). 'Strong' fits the general claim. For the user's specific kind of utterance, 'moderate' is more accurate.
  M stretch_but_reasonable: COUNTER-EVIDENCE: Gratitude 'finds, reminds and binds', and positivity is itself a core friendship maintenance behavior || The direction is right. But Algoe et al. (2008) measured FELT gratitude for received benefits, not expressed positivity. Oswald et al.'s 'positivity' is a general cheerful demeanor, not closeness labels, and the data are cross-sectional. Lambert et al. (2010) is the most relevant study, and may have included expressions to friends (check this). Present it as 'positive expression belongs to normal friendship maintenance', not as direct evidence that superlatives or closeness statements help.
  M stretch_but_reasonable: COUNTER-EVIDENCE with a real-dynamic twist: capitalization (sharing good things), where the listener's response determines the effect || The base finding is sound and well replicated. Gable et al. (2004) also found that passive-constructive (muted) responses were not beneficial, which directly supports the mismatch twist. The extrapolation from sharing personal good news to statements about the relationship itself is untested. The mismatch idea should be tied to the self-disclosure reciprocity norm: an intimate statement implicitly invites a matching reply, and an unmatched one feels like rejection. This is one of the most useful mechanisms for the user, because it moves attention from 'did I speak?' to 'how did they respond?'.
  M stretch_but_reasonable: Form matters: specific, other-praising, present-focused appreciation vs. global relationship labels and superlatives || The pieces are uneven. Baxter & Wilmot (relationship talk is taboo, especially in cross-sex friendships) and the self-disclosure literature give a reasonable basis for 'we've gotten really close' carrying a real interpersonal cost: it raises questions about definition, obligation or romantic intent. Algoe et al. (2016) supports other-praising, but in couples. Three pieces are overreach as support: Eastwick et al. (selectivity is not specificity), Aronson & Linder (the evaluation is OF the participant, involves real reversals to negative, and the loss effect was not significant), and Brummelman et al. (children's challenge-seeking). Better anchors: expectancy violations theory (Burgoon), where affection beyond what the stage of the relationship leads someone to expect can be judged negatively depending on how it is interpreted; Collins & Miller's (1994) meta-analysis, where disclosure aimed specifically at the recipient produces more liking; and social penetration's 'too intimate too soon'. Keep 'weak'.
  MISSING: Comparison level (Thibaut & Kelley, 1959; interdependence theory) is the relationship-science version of the reference-point mechanism. Satisfaction depends on outcomes relative to a comparison level shaped by salient past outcomes. Cite it alongside or instead of consumer-satisfaction research when talking about friendships.
  MISSING: Relational dialectics: friendships continually balance connection against autonomy, and expressiveness against protectiveness (Rawlins, 1992, Friendship Matters; Baxter & Montgomery, 1996; Altman, Vinsel & Brown, 1981, on cycles of openness and closedness). After a phase of intense closeness, partners often pull back a little to restore autonomy. An explicit 'we've gotten so close' can make the connection pole salient and prompt that rebalancing. This is a real, non-mystical friendship-specific explanation for a dip in the following days, and it is missing from this cluster.
  MISSING: Attachment avoidance and fear of intimacy: friends high in attachment avoidance tend to respond to explicit intimacy with distancing ('deactivating' strategies; Mikulincer & Shaver, Attachment in Adulthood). An explicit closeness label can therefore cause real short-term withdrawal in some friends. That is about the friend's comfort with closeness, not about the statement being cursed.
  MISSING: Expectancy violations theory (Burgoon, 1978; Burgoon & Hale, 1988) and affection-exchange research (Floyd, 2006; Erbert & Floyd, 2004, on affectionate expressions as face-threatening acts; check the details): affection that goes beyond what the relationship's stage leads someone to expect creates uncertainty and can be judged negatively, especially when it raises questions of romantic intent. This is directly relevant to cross-sex friendships (see also Afifi & Burgoon, 1998, 'We never talk about that', Personal Relationships, on topic avoidance in cross-sex friendships).
  MISSING: The self-disclosure-liking meta-analysis (Collins & Miller, 1994, Psychological Bulletin, 116(3), 457-475): disclosure produces liking, and disclosure aimed specifically at the recipient produces more liking. This supports the 'form matters' advice better than Eastwick et al. The self-disclosure reciprocity norm explains why an intimate statement that gets no matching reply stings.

  MISSING: The beautiful mess effect (Bruk, Scholl & Bless, 2018, JPSP 115(2), 192-205): people judge their own displays of vulnerability more harshly than observers do.   MISSING: Positive illusions are self-fulfilling in the GOOD direction: idealizing a relationship predicts more satisfaction and stability (Murray, Holmes & Griffin, 1996, JPSP 70(1) and 71(6), 'love is not blind, but prescient'). This is important counter-evidence to 'high hopes jinx things'.
  MISSING: Tempting-fate research explains why the jinx feeling is so compelling even to people who do not believe in it: after tempting fate, the bad outcome is easier to imagine and seems more likely (Risen & Gilovich, 2008, JPSP 95(2), 293-307; Risen, 2016, Psychological Review, on 'acquiescing' to superstitious intuitions).
